The Xoloitzcuintli as a Solar Entity: Physiology, Benefits of Ultraviolet Radiation and Its Connection to Tonalli in Nahua Cosmology

This English editorial adaptation preserves the original research's assertions and themes. Read the original Spanish manuscript.

Introduction: Biotype, Cultural Significance and Preservation

The Xoloitzcuintli is an exceptional zootechnical and cultural phenomenon in which evolutionary biology, veterinary dermatology and Mesoamerican anthropology converge. A biocultural monument with more than 3,500 years of history and a living heritage of Mexico City, this dog occupies a place in pre-Hispanic mythical and religious thought. For Xolos Ramírez, understanding the breed means exploring both its physiology and the metaphysical web surrounding it. As explored in Café con Xolos Ramírez through the puppy Tonatiuh, the modern Xoloitzcuintli is a living pyramid walking among us.

This research examines two interconnected dimensions: mechanisms of canine biology, systemic benefits attributed to heliotherapy and the toxicological risks of ultraviolet exposure; and Nahua cosmology as reflected in the work of Alfredo López Austin, the Florentine Codex compiled by Bernardino de Sahagún, and the Legend of the Suns in the Codex Chimalpopoca. Its central interpretation is that a biological search for solar warmth was understood as an expression of Tonalli, a solar and animating force, associated with the dog's role as a guide in Mictlán and with traditional healing. In the Mesoamerican imagination, the dog did more than absorb warmth for thermoregulation: it could be understood as a living transmuter of cosmic energy.

Genetic Physiology and the Dermatological Architecture of the Xoloitzcuintli

Hairlessness is attributed in the source research to a highly conserved genetic mutation affecting ectoderm development in embryogenesis, described as a dominant trait lethal in the homozygous state. Hairless individuals are heterozygous, whereas embryos with two copies of the dominant allele are reabsorbed before birth. Hairless and coated puppies may therefore occur naturally in the same litter; coated dogs carry the same wider breed heritage.

Because the embryonic ectoderm gives rise to skin, hair follicles, sweat glands, nervous-system structures and dental enamel, the hairless phenotype is associated with ectodermal dysplasia and characteristic congenital absence of incisors, premolars and sometimes canine teeth. This missing dentition is treated as a normal feature of the hairless variety in the source text.

Without a fur coat's thermal insulation, heat transfers directly across the skin. The manuscript argues that the hairless Xoloitzcuintli's basal temperature tends to the higher end of the canine range: approximately 38.3–39.5°C, versus approximately 38.0–39.2°C for coated dogs, with even warmer peripheral regions. This tactile warmth and the absence of a coat are described as reasons for active sun-seeking to maintain comfort and thermal homeostasis.

Systemic Veterinary Benefits of Canine Heliotherapy

The original research describes 15–20 minutes of daily sun exposure as a physiologically normal interval and associates controlled heliotherapy with dermatological, musculoskeletal, neurological and systemic benefits.

Vitamin D Synthesis and Intestinal Mineral Homeostasis

The manuscript argues that UVB radiation contributes to the formation of vitamin D precursors in canine skin and sebaceous secretions, which dogs then ingest during grooming. It acknowledges that dogs synthesize vitamin D far less efficiently than primates. Vitamin D is presented as essential for intestinal calcium and phosphorus absorption, mineralization, muscle contraction and immune function, with relevance to orthopedic problems including medial patellar luxation in miniature or toy dogs.

Neurochemistry, Circadian Regulation and Mood

According to the source, natural daylight activates visual and hypothalamic-pineal pathways and is associated with serotonin release, emotional calm and reduced separation anxiety, hyperexcitability and stress. The text links prolonged daylight deprivation to disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and elevated cortisol, potentially contributing to lethargy, depression-like behaviors and stereotypies. It also describes daylight as a circadian synchronizer that suppresses daytime melatonin and supports nighttime release, sleep quality and the relaxed behavior observed when dogs sunbathe.

Musculoskeletal Therapy in Older Dogs and Joint Relief

The article attributes peripheral vasodilation, increased regional circulation, oxygen delivery and muscular relaxation to radiant solar warmth. For older dogs with osteoarthritis, spondylosis and other degenerative joint conditions, it presents controlled warmth as a therapeutic aid for stiffness, inflammation and range of motion, potentially reducing reliance on nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medication.

Skin Microbiome Modulation and Antibacterial Properties

The manuscript describes UV radiation as bactericidal, virucidal and fungicidal, and presents moderate sunlight as a possible way to dry moisture in skin folds and inhibit microbial proliferation. It relates this to comedones, debris accumulation and juvenile acne in hairless Xoloitzcuintli, emphasizing the need to remain below the threshold of thermal and cellular injury.

Dermatological Risks, Oncological Progression and Solar Toxicology

The absence of dense fur leaves exposed skin vulnerable to UVA-related photoaging and UVB-associated sunburn. The source identifies lightly pigmented areas, the nose, ear edges, abdomen, groin and scars as particularly susceptible, noting environmental UV index readings above 3 as a risk context.

Solar Dermatitis, Actinic Keratosis and Squamous Cell Carcinoma

The research describes a progression from redness, dryness, itching and flaking to UV-induced oxidative DNA damage and actinic keratosis, characterized by thickened plaques, crusting, fissures and persistent ulcers. It further warns that untreated premalignant changes can progress to locally aggressive squamous cell carcinoma requiring substantial veterinary treatment or surgery.

Heatstroke and Systemic Hyperthermia

The manuscript explains that severe heat, poor ventilation and midday to afternoon sun can overwhelm thermoregulation. Temperatures exceeding 41.5–42°C are associated in the text with life-threatening heatstroke. Excessive panting, tachycardia, thick saliva, changing mucous-membrane color, disorientation, weakness, vomiting, ataxia, collapse, coagulopathy and organ failure are among the listed warning signs, requiring immediate cooling and emergency veterinary attention.

Care Protocols, Supplementation and Sunscreen Toxicology

The article calls for physical and topical sun protection in hairless dogs and warns against indiscriminate use of human sunscreens, especially because dogs may lick topical products.

Severe Zinc Oxide Ingestion Toxicity

The original research warns that ingesting high concentrations of zinc oxide in human mineral sunscreens or diaper creams can lead to serious zinc toxicity. It describes absorption in the stomach and systemic effects on erythrocytes, Heinz bodies, hemolytic anemia, dark urine, jaundice, vomiting, diarrhea, weakness, kidney injury and pancreatitis; treatment may require intravenous fluids and blood transfusions. It also advises avoiding PABA and salicylate-based sunscreen ingredients due to possible gastrointestinal, hepatic and nervous-system toxicity.
